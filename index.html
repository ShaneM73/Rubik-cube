The cube as matrices, and as a group

Every panel below is computed live from the cube on the left. Turn a face, or press U D L R F B on your keyboard (hold Shift for the reverse turn).

The idea in one minute

The obvious way to model a cube is a list of 54 stickers plus big tables saying where each sticker goes on each move. This model replaces all of that with one small vector and one 3×3 matrix per cubelet.

stateEach cubelet is a pair (c, R): who it is, and how it has been rotated. moveA dot product picks the nine cubelets of a face; one matrix product turns them. testOne equation per cubelet says whether it is solved.

Read the cards in order. Each one shows its formula with live numbers for the cubelet outlined in pink. Click another cubelet to change it. This model describes the cube and checks it; it does not tell you which move to make. The Solve tab does that.

1A cubelet is a vector

Inside the cube is a rigid cross that holds the six centre pieces. They spin in place but never travel, so they fix a coordinate frame: origin at the core, x through front and back, y through right and left, z through top and bottom.

Core: a six-armed cross. It is the only part that never moves. Centres: each is screwed onto one arm with a spring, so it can spin but not leave. Edges: a tab on the back hooks behind the two neighbouring centres. Corners: a foot on the back is trapped behind the three neighbouring edges.

This is a simplified cartoon of the mechanism: real parts are curved so the layers can slide, and the arms do not stretch.

A 3×3×3 block has 27 positions. Each gets the integer vector c of its place in the solved cube, with entries −1, 0 or 1. The middle one, (0, 0, 0), is the hidden core, which leaves 26 cubelets. The vector c never changes. It is the cubelet's name.

The 1-norm ‖c‖₁ = |x| + |y| + |z| counts the non-zero entries, and that is exactly the number of stickers.

Centres‖c‖₁ = 1, e.g. (0, 0, 1)6 pieces Edges‖c‖₁ = 2, e.g. (1, 0, 1)12 pieces Corners‖c‖₁ = 3, e.g. (1, 1, 1)8 pieces

2Colours are basis vectors

A colour is not a label or a string. It is the unit vector of the face where that colour's centre sits. Six colours, six signed basis vectors.

This is why the model needs no colour bookkeeping: a sticker that faces direction +ex when solved simply is green.

3A configuration is the pair (c, R)

Every one of the 26 cubelets carries its own three things.

cIts home address. Fixed forever. RIts own 3×3 rotation matrix: the total rotation it has undergone since the cube was solved. pWhere it is right now. Not stored, but computed: p = R·c.

Where R comes from. Nobody chooses it. It starts as the identity I, so at first p = I·c = c and the cubelet is at home. Each time a face turns and this cubelet is on that face, its matrix becomes M·R, where M is that turn. Otherwise it is left alone. After many moves, R is the product of all the turns that carried this cubelet, newest on the left.

Position is not the whole story. After those two turns the corner is back at (1, 1, 1), yet R is not the identity: the corner is in its home spot, twisted. So knowing p and c does not tell you R. A corner fits any spot in three ways and an edge in two.

Stickers fill the gap. The columns of diag(c) are the cubelet's stickers, as directions. Multiply by R and each column shows where that sticker points now. The bar above a column is the sticker's colour.

4A move is a dot product, then a matrix product

A face is named by its unit normal v, the arrow pointing out of it. For the top face v = (0, 0, 1).

Select. The dot product v·p reads off one coordinate of p: how far the cubelet sits along that face's axis. It is 1 on that face, 0 in the middle slice and −1 on the opposite face. So a cubelet turns exactly when v·p = v·(R·c) > 0, and nine cubelets always pass.

Turn. Each selected cubelet updates with one multiplication, R ← M·R, where M is a quarter turn about v. That single line replaces the sticker tables.

Hover a move button to preview which cubelets pass the test.

Why centres stay put. A rotation leaves its own axis unchanged: M·v = v. A vector that a matrix leaves unchanged is called an eigenvector with eigenvalue 1. The centre of the turning face has c = v, so its position M·c = c does not change.

5Solved means R·diag(c) = diag(c)

The cube is solved when every sticker faces its own colour's direction. That is one equation per cubelet.

The eigenvector reading. Take the equation one column at a time. It says R·(sticker direction) = sticker direction for each sticker: every sticker direction must be an eigenvector of R with eigenvalue 1. How strict that is depends on the number of stickers.

Centre1 direction fixedany spin about that axis passes Edge2 directions fixedonly R = I passes Corner3 directions fixedonly R = I passes

Why not simply ask for R = I? That would be too strict for centres. A centre spins about its own axis on every turn of its face, so its R is rarely the identity, yet its single sticker still points the right way. Select a centre and turn its face to see this.

The twisted corner again. In the example from card 3, R·(1,1,1) = (1,1,1), so c itself is an eigenvector and the piece is in its place. But R·(1,0,0) = (0,0,1): the green sticker now points up, so the test fails. Twists and flips are caught with no extra flags.

This is a test, not a solver. It tells you whether you are done, never what to do next. For that, see the Solve tab.

1Moves form a group

Do one move after another and you get another scramble (closure). Doing nothing is the identity. Every move can be undone (inverses). Those three facts make the set of reachable states a group G, generated by the six face turns.

To undo a word, reverse it and invert each letter: (ab)⁻¹ = b⁻¹a⁻¹.

current wordits inverse

2Order matters, and every word has an order

The group is not commutative: R U and U R land in different states. The commutator R U R′ U′ measures the difference. If the two turns commuted it would do nothing.

Because G is finite, repeating any word eventually returns to where you started. The number of repeats is the word's order.

3What no move can change

Three quantities stay fixed under every face turn. They are computed here straight from the matrices R.

Take the cube apart and reassemble it wrongly to break them. No sequence of turns will repair the damage. Only the same pop-out trick will.

4Counting the group

Reassembling freely gives 8! · 3⁸ · 12! · 2¹² ≈ 5.19 × 10²⁰ cubes. The three invariants cut that into 3 × 2 × 2 = 12 separate families that turns cannot connect, so

|G| = 8! · 3⁷ · 12! · 2¹⁰ = 43,252,003,274,489,856,000

5Where the two views meet

The matrices R are never arbitrary. Each one is among the 24 rotations of a cube, which themselves form a group (the same shape as the permutations of four objects). A cube state is a list of 26 of these matrices, and a move multiplies nine of them on the left by M.

So the linear-algebra model is a concrete representation of G: group elements become matrices, and composing moves becomes matrix multiplication. The invariants in card 3 are functions of those matrices that every M leaves alone.

Can I factor each R back into moves and solve the cube that way?

For one cubelet, yes, and easily: there are only 24 possible rotation matrices and each is a product of a few quarter turns. If cubelets could be fixed one at a time the puzzle would be trivial.

The catch is that a move never acts on one cubelet. One turn multiplies the same M into nine cubelets at once, and which nine depends on where everything currently is. You need a single sequence of moves such that, for all 20 movable cubelets together, the turns each one happened to ride along with undo its own R. Fixing one piece by its own short factorisation scrambles the eight that came along.

The per-cubelet factorisation is still useful as a lower bound. If some cubelet needs two quarter turns to get home, the whole cube needs at least two moves. Search-based solvers use estimates like this to decide which states to try first.

So is this a solver, or only a test?

Only a description and a test. The model supplies three things: a state (c, R) per cubelet, a rule for applying a move, and a test for solved. None of them says which move to make next.

A solver is something built on top. A program searches: it tries moves, uses the rule to see where each leads, and uses the test to know when to stop. A person uses a memorised method, which is what the Solve tab teaches. Its building blocks come from the group-theory side: commutators and set-up moves that disturb only a few pieces.

Is there exactly one shortest solution?

Usually not. A cube one turn from solved has a single shortest solution, but most states have several. Opposite faces commute (R L = L R), so many shortest solutions come in swapped pairs, and there are often entirely different sequences of the same length.

Every state can be solved in at most 20 moves when a half turn counts as one move, and in at most 26 when only quarter turns count. Finding a shortest solution takes a computer search that deepens one move at a time and discards branches using lower bounds. Hand methods give up shortness for something memorable and take 100 moves or more.

A method you can do by hand

The guide solves the cube in seven stages, from the yellow bottom layer up to the white top layer. The rule that makes this work: once a stage is finished, every later sequence is chosen so that it gives the finished part back unchanged.

Early on you have lots of free space, so short moves do the job. Near the end there is no free space left, so the sequences get longer, because they must lift the solved layers out of the way and put them back.

The guide reads the cube as it is now. Scramble it, turn it yourself, or follow along, and it always shows the next step from the current state. It aims for clarity, not the fewest moves: expect about 140 turns for a full scramble.

Reading the moves

A letter alone is a quarter turn clockwise as you look straight at that face. An apostrophe means counter-clockwise. A 2 means a half turn. Keep the cube in the same grip the whole time: green toward you, white on top.
